FAHNES: Feature-Aware Hypergraph Generation via Next-Scale Prediction

Created in June 02, 2025

2025   ·   machine-learning   generative-models   graph-theory   hypergraphs   ·   papers

This blog post summarizes our paper, FAHNES: Feature-Aware Hypergraph Generation via Next-Scale Prediction, which expands our work HYGENE: A Diffusion-based Hypergraph Generation Method.

TL;DR: We introduce a framework able to generate hypergraphs with node and hyperedge features, based on an “unzoom-zoom” principle. First, we compute increasingly coarse versions of a hypergraph (“unzoom”), keeping track of the number of nodes in each supernode/superhyperedge and averaging features. Then, we train a model to reconstruct the original details from these simplified versions (“zoom”). Generation is achieved by repeatedly applying the trained model to zoom in, gradually increasing detail until the desired hypergraph size is reached.

Preliminaries

A random graph and a random hypergraph.

Traditional graphs consist of nodes connected by edges, with each edge linking precisely two nodes. Hypergraphs, however, offer a more versatile framework. In a hypergraph, edges — referred to as hyperedges — can connect an arbitrary number of nodes simultaneously. This generalization allows hypergraphs to capture higher-order and more nuanced relationships compared to standard graphs. This also makes them much more expressive, for instance:

  • 3D meshes can be represented with triangles as 3-edges
  • Electronic circuits can be modeled with routers replaced by hyperedges connecting all associated components
  • Molecules can be depicted more accurately, with functional groups like carboxylic acids joined into hyperedges to highlight their higher-order relationship

Our work aims to develop a deep-learning based method capable of generating new samples from a given distribution of featured hypergraphs. For instance, given examples of 3D meshes representing variants of an object, we want to train a model that can generate new instances. Generating topology was already addressed with HYGENE: A Diffusion-based Hypergraph Generation Method, so here we focus on improving the quality of generated topology and adding support for features.

Examples of datasets we tested our method against

In other words, given a dataset of featured hypergraphs (with 3D positions, component types, or other characteristics) that share similar topological properties, our goal is to train a model capable of generating new hypergraphs with matching properties.

Main inspirations

FlowAR: Scale-wise Autoregressive Image Generation Meets Flow Matching

Beginning with a single pixel, at every generation step FlowAR upsample the current image (i.e. replace each pixel by a 2x2 block with the same value) that will serve as conditioning for the next round of flow-matching: starting from a gaussian noise, the model iteratively denoises the image to generate the final output, while conditioned on the upsampled prediction from the previous step.

HYGENE: A Diffusion-based Hypergraph Generation Method

Our method for hypergraph generation builds on our previous work HYGENE: A Diffusion-based Hypergraph Generation Method, which uses an “unzoom–zoom” scheme:

1. Hypergraph Downsampling (Unzoom)

To enable coarsening on hypergraphs, we project them into graph space using two complementary representations:

  • Clique Expansion: Each hyperedge is replaced with a clique of edges weighted by \(1/ \vert e \vert\), where \(\vert e \vert\) is the hyperedge size. This preserves spectral properties, allowing us to apply traditional coarsening methods.

  • Star Expansion (Bipartite View): The hypergraph is represented as a bipartite graph. One set of nodes corresponds to original hypergraph nodes, the other to hyperedges, with links connecting nodes to the hyperedges they belong to.

We apply a spectrum-preserving coarsening algorithm to the clique expansion to produce a hierarchy of coarsened graphs. The same node merges are mirrored in the bipartite representation, and hyperedges are merged when their node sets become identical (i.e. the same hyperedge appears twice).

Coarsening: (a) Compute the weighted clique expansion by collapsing each hyperedge into an appropriately weighted clique. (b) Coarsen the clique expansion while preserving the spectral properties of the hypergraph (dark blue nodes). (c) Update the bipartite view: corresponding left side nodes (in dark blue) are merged, then right side nodes representing the same hyperedge (circled in black) are merged.

2. Hypergraph Upsampling (Zoom)

From a reduced bipartite graph, we train a neural network to reverse the coarsening process:

  • Expansion: Predicts which nodes and hyperedges should be duplicated at each step. New entities inherit connections from their parent nodes/hyperedges.

  • Refinement: Filters the resulting edges to ensure structural similarity.

For generation, starting with a single node and single hyperedge, we apply this expansion + refinement step iteratively to recover a full-size hypergraph.

Our model starts from a single pair of linked nodes (in the bipartite representation) and iteratively expands the left-side nodes (in dark blue) and right-side nodes (in red), where each duplicate keeps the connections of its parent node. Then, our method refines the resulting bipartite graph filtering edges to recover an appropriate local structure.

Our Work

FlowAR can generate features but is unable to generate topology. On the other hand, HYGENE can generate topology but cannot generate features. Our work combines both to allows generating featured hypergraphs for various modalities. Our contributions are the addition of a node-budget mechanism, representing the size of each cluster (i.e. how many nodes it contains), and the integration of hierarchical generation to our setting.

Coarsening Sequence / Downsampling

During coarsening, every node and hyperedge starts with a node budget of one (coarsening has not started yet, so each “cluster” represents a single node, itself) ; then, for each merging of clusters, the resulting supernode / superhyperedge inherits the sum of the node budgets of the nodes / hyperedges it contains. Node/hyperedge features are averaged when merging several nodes, the weights in the averaging being the node budgets. That way, each cluster has as feature the average of all the nodes it contains. During generation, children of a node / hyperegde obtain the same feature as their parent (“coarse features”), and the model is tasked with predicting the new features (“refined features”).

Coarsening Inversion / Upsampling

During inversion of the coarsening, in order to have a constrained space for the predictions of node budgets, the model is not tasked to directly generate the resulting node budgets for all children of a cluster, but rather tasked with predicting the splitting of node budgets. Indeed, during training, we task it to predict the ratio between the ground truth value for the refined node budget and the parent node budget. That way, predictions are constrained in [0, 1], which helps learning as it reduces the variance of target outputs. Additionally, to ensure that input variance is also controlled, we use a sinusoidal positional encoding to transform the integers.

For features, the process is done similarly as for hierarchical image generation. First, the children nodes of a cluster are first given the same feature as their parent (i.e. we have a “pixelated” version of the hypergraph). Then, based on these coarse features, the model enhances and refines the features (i.e. it computes the clear hypergraph from its pixelated version).

The following figure explains our strategy.

Combining everything gives the above pipeline: i) During training, input hypergraphs are progressively _coarsened_ by merging nodes and hyperedges, yielding a multiscale representation. Node features are averaged during merging, and budgets are summed. ii) The model is trained to predict which nodes were merged at each scale. iii) In the _expansion_ phase, merged nodes are expanded (shown in dark), inheriting their parent’s features, budget, and connectivity. The model is trained to (a) identify which edges should be removed (dotted lines), (b) predict how the parent’s budget should be split across the children (instead of predicting absolute values), and (c) refine the features of newly expanded nodes (_refinement_).

Limitations

Results for molecule generate show that our method is not competitive compared to one-shot graph prediction methods, unlike hierarchical image generation which is stronger than regular one-shot methods. We hypothesize that the difference stems from the discrete nature of the generation process: for continuous predictions with fixed topology (like for images), the “latent space” is “continuous”, in the sense that nodes (i.e. pixels) of slightly different images have slightly different representations. Thus a small error during a prediction step results in a slightly different starting point. This is not true in our discrete setting, due to the tresholding effect of either adding an edge or removing it, expanding a cluster or not: a small error at a prediction step results in a different topology, which changes a lot the underlying embedding (with a GNN, either the information passes or not). One-shot models do not suffer from that, as the topology (a fully connected graph) is fixed during generation.

Conclusion

This work represents a significant first step towards featured hierarchical (hyper)graph generation. Our future research will focus on rendering the framework more robust to perturbations / prediction errors.
